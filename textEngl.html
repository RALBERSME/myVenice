Read the text out loud to me:



Piero approached the guest, who stood by the window with hunched shoulders, staring out at the lagoon as if there were absolutely nothing to see. The old cameriere already held his order pad in hand, but he hesitated. The furrows on the man’s brow were no ordinary lines of worry; they looked deeply etched into his skin.

“Prego, please, do take a seat,” Piero said softly. His voice carried that warm, paternal tone he had cultivated over decades behind the bar. He gestured toward a small oak table. “From here, you have the finest view in all of Venice, sir.”

The stranger—perhaps in his early forties, wearing a simple plaid shirt and dark flannel trousers—slid into the chair Piero held out for him. He moved heavily, as though every limb required immense effort. He ordered an espresso.

As Piero frothed milk, arranged cornetti, and tended to the morning rush, his gaze kept drifting back to the window table. The man was sitting exactly as before. Head resting heavily in his hands, he stared down into the small cup. He hadn't touched it once in the past half hour.

Piero set the bread knife aside. He wiped his hands on the white bistro apron which, as always, he wore low on his hips so that it nearly brushed his ankles, and stepped out from behind the bar. With slow steps, he approached the table and stood beside it.

“A beautiful day today,” he began gently. “You can hear the tourists laughing all the way in here. Aren't you tempted to go out into the sun and enjoy life a little?”

The guest slowly raised his head. His eyelids seemed infinitely heavy, the corners of his mouth turned down in sheer exhaustion. A deep, paralyzing weariness lay across his face.

“You’re not here for the gondolas, are you?” Piero asked quietly.

“I only came because of the gondolas.” The man pulled a crumpled handkerchief from his pocket and dabbed his forehead.

“I don't understand.” Piero drew up a chair and sat down with him. “Then why sit so unhappily in a dark dining room? Why aren't you out there with the others?”

“I’m looking for a specific woman. And I can't find her anywhere.”

“Ah, a matter of the heart.” A spark of sympathy flashed in Piero’s eyes. “It’s peak season, my friend. The alleys are overflowing with people. Do you know where she lives? Or what her name is?”

“That’s just it. I don’t know her at all.” The guest looked at Piero helplessly. The cameriere shook his head, at a loss.

“I would truly love to help you. But that sounds like an impossibility.”

“You think I’m crazy, don’t you?” The man let out a heavy sigh. “Perhaps I am. You see, I had a terrible accident. I spent months in a coma, hooked up to machines. None of the doctors believed I would ever wake up. I was like someone buried alive, completely frozen.” He pressed his hands flat against both cheeks, squeezing them tightly to demonstrate the suffocating confinement.

“Just like that. I couldn’t move, couldn't turn my head a single millimeter. My own body felt entirely alien, like it belonged to a corpse. But my hearing was intact. And through that hearing, all the cruelty of the world filtered through to me. ‘There’s no need to keep coming,’ the doctors told my relatives. ‘He can’t hear you anymore. His eyes may be open, but they see nothing.’ They said that even if I woke up by some miracle, I’d remain an invalid. If I ever made it into a wheelchair, that would be a success. They told them to face the fact that I would never come home again.” The guest swallowed hard and pressed the handkerchief against his eyes.

“You have no idea what it feels like to have to hear such things about yourself. Inside, I had already given up. If it's just going to be a vegetative existence, then please let it be over, I prayed. I just wanted to stop breathing. But those machines didn’t care about my will. They relentlessly kept pumping air into my lungs. Mercilessly.”

Piero’s face had lost all color. He sat leaning far forward, his mouth slightly open, listening as spellbound as if his own life depended on the stranger's words.

“All sense of time was gone. I don't know how many weeks I spent in that absolute blackness. But at some point, something shifted. Light began to dawn behind my eyes. At first, it was just faint gray veils, then they grew denser, and finally, the first outlines emerged. My body was still as rigid as a piece of dry wood, but inside my head, the world was returning. First I saw a golden shimmer, then blue. Everything was terribly distorted, but with each passing day, new colors appeared.”

The guest paused. His hand trembled slightly as he reached for the cup and took a small sip of the now ice-cold espresso.

“The patches of color slowly came together,” the guest continued, his voice dropping to a whisper. “It was a painting. It hung on the bare wall right above the foot of my bed. I saw the Venetian lagoon, the gondolas, the tourists. And right in the middle of it, a young woman with dark brown hair. She wore a light sunhat, with her curls spilling out from underneath.”

The man’s fingers drifted to his mouth. Piero saw that they were shaking violently. Then, the guest wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

“The woman in the painting smiled at me. She held her hand out to me, and I swear to you, I could hear her. At first, it was just isolated words, fragments. But eventually, we had real conversations. For hours, as long as the sun shone into my room. She told me about Italy. About her father, who ran a restaurant in Treviso, and about so many other things. Over and over, she told me the same sentence: ‘Don’t give up. Every little thing grows big one day. That is what my father taught me, and it is the greatest truth in the world. It applies to you, too, my friend.’”

Piero moved as if in a trance. He slid his large hands, lined by decades of hard labor, across the table and placed them over the guest’s.

“That hospital,” the old waiter whispered, his voice cracking, “the painting... where is it?”

“It’s no longer there. I bought it from the clinic.” The man reached into his back pocket and pulled out a smartphone. His thumbs danced across the glass until the gallery opened with a soft chime. He handed the screen to Piero.

The image showed exactly what he had described. But Piero’s gaze was instantly arrested by the face of the young woman beneath the straw hat.

In that moment, something broke inside the old cameriere. Tears welled up in his eyes, unbidden and hot. He pulled the smartphone close to his face, staring at the pixels, and desperately pressed his lips against the display, again and over again, as if he could break through the coldness of the glass.

„È un miracolo“Piero murmured. He stood up, took a step back, and held the smartphone against his chest like a sacred relic, shaking his head in disbelief. “It is a miracle.”

The guest looked at him motionlessly, utterly overwhelmed.

“That is my daughter,” Piero said. A brilliance lit up his eyes, entirely vanished of the weariness from moments before. He pointed a trembling finger through the empty room. “Do you see that painting over there, above the counter? My brother Beppo painted that. He made dozens of oil paintings of her.”

The guest turned his head and looked across the dining room toward the wall. A shiver ran down his spine. Hanging there was almost the exact same motif. It was merely painted from a slightly different perspective—the young woman was visible from much closer.

“She is beautiful,” the guest said softly. He closed his eyes for a moment to process what he had seen.

“She was,” Piero replied, handing the smartphone back. He looked at the floor, his voice turning hoarse again. “She died nearly thirty years ago. Giving birth to my first grandson. Her husband and I had to bury her.” He swallowed. “That was a dark time. I felt so utterly powerless, cheated by life. I hated this world because it had taken the dearest thing I possessed.”

The old waiter slowly sat back down with him. For a while, only the distant murmur of the city filled the room.

“Emanuela was her name. She was my only child,” Piero continued, his gaze fixed on nothingness. “But I had made her a promise, and that is what ultimately saved me. Back then, I still had that little place in Treviso. We call it Little Venice because of the many canals in the old town. Emanuela always longed for the real city on the lagoon. On my days off, we often drove out here, and that’s where Beppo’s paintings were born. Whenever business was bad, I would take my little girl in my arms and say: ‘Every little thing grows big one day, Emanuela. Eventually, we will open a restaurant in the real Venice.’ After her death, I couldn’t bear it in Treviso anymore. I risked everything, took out a massive loan, and bought this place. I worked myself to the bone. And the more guests came, the louder the laughter grew, the more life returned to my own heart. And now, look around. It turned into a good restaurant, didn't it?”

A sudden surge of pride flared up in the old man. But seeing the guest threaten to sink back into his rigid posture, Piero tapped firmly on the wooden table. “Come now. What is your name?”

“Felix,” the man stammered, slightly startled.

“Felix. The lucky one,” Piero translated, and a genuine smile spread across his sun-bronzed face. He placed a hand on Felix’s shoulder. “And isn't it the greatest luck of all? My daughter has been dead for three decades, yet she saved your life. Without her, you wouldn’t be sitting at my table today. We must toast to that.”

Without waiting for an answer, Piero went behind the bar. Seconds later, a cork popped. With an almost youthful lightness, he returned, balancing a silver tray with two foaming glasses.

“You’re right,” Felix said, and for the first time, a faint glimmer of peace reflected in his eyes. “Without her, I wouldn't be here anymore. Her words carried me through the darkness.” He looked at the old cameriere. “Do you think... do you think I just imagined it all? Was it just the fever in the coma?”

Piero handed him the glass.

“You weren't hallucinating, my friend. I speak to her every day, too. Her spirit never truly left.”

“Do you really believe that?”

Piero looked at him steadfastly. “Emanuela means: God is with us. And do you truly believe He would have abandoned us in this moment?”

Felix’s face relaxed, his features softening. He took the glass and took a sip of the cool, sparkling wine. He had come to Venice to search for an unreachable woman in a painting—and only now realized that he had never lost her.
